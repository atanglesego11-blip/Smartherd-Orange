SmartHerd.ai

A herder picks the sick animal out of two hundred at fifty metres, before any lesion. He is reading which one lags behind, which one stands apart, which one is not eating when the others eat. That skill is not written down anywhere, and fewer people are learning it. So we wrote it down, and taught it to a collar.

The toggle is the experiment. Tick “hide temperature and pulse” and you are looking at what a GPS-only tracker sees. A sick animal standing still and a healthy animal resting in the midday shade become the same thing, because in movement data they are the same thing. Illness detection falls from an F1 of 0.993 to 0.911.

What the pulse buys you

Per-class F1 on held-out animals — animals the model never trained on, not merely windows it never saw. A random forest on summary statistics is reported alongside, because on a benchmark this clean a classical method is competitive and hiding that would be the easiest thing to catch us on.

The knowledge is the feature set

Every channel the model reads was chosen because a herder already uses it. This is an extract; the full table, with its verification status and its list of signs we know matter and cannot yet measure, is in the repository.

What a herder watchesWhat the collar measures
Go sala morago — it goes behind, arrives last at waterdistance to the herd centroid, and its trend across hours
It stands apart from the others at restthe same channel, read during rest hours
It does not graze when the others grazespeed and activity during grazing hours — only separable from shade-resting when read with temperature and pulse
It walks badly, short hesitant stepsstep-length distribution and turning variance inside a window
Breathing fast while standing stillheart rate decoupled from movement
One or two leave in a straight line at nightsustained speed, near-zero turning variance, hour of day, distance past the boundary
The whole herd moving fast in daylight is normal — it is being driven to the dip tanksame kinematics, separated by herd cohesion staying tight and by time of day
It stops chewing the cudnot yet measurable. Needs a jaw or neck accelerometer. Recorded as a gap rather than dropped.

The dip-tank row is the one that matters most. A system built without it sends a theft alarm every dipping day, the farmer stops reading the alarms, and the one real theft goes unanswered. That distinction is not in any dataset you can download.

What is real here, and what is not

Every number on this page comes from simulated animals. There is no field data in this build and we do not pretend otherwise. The generator is published, seeded, and grounded in the SADC animal-disease record, published physiological baselines, and the herder indicators above.

It is also deliberately hard. Our first version scored 99% on everything, which meant the task was fake. We rebuilt it with the three things that make this difficult in real life: healthy animals that stand in the shade for hours, legitimate herd drives that look exactly like rustling, and subclinical cases carrying almost no fever.

These figures show that the architecture can learn the signal we encoded. They are not field accuracy, and nothing in the repository calls them that. They become field accuracy when the same script is re-run, unchanged, on real collar data — which is step three of the roadmap.

The hardware is simulated too, in Proteus VSM: an ATmega328P with an LM35 for temperature and a potentiometer standing in for the pulse front end, emitting the same telemetry line as the real collar. Sweep the potentiometer while the animal stays still and you can watch the classifier change its mind. That bench cannot measure power, and an LM35 on a 10-bit ADC quantises to about half a degree, so we say so.

SmartHerd.ai — open-source core, MIT licensed. 33,382 parameters, 68 seconds to train on one CPU core. No GPU, no paid API, no cloud service.

Disease data: SADC animal-disease record, 2011 annex (78 diseases). Wildlife context: Statistics Botswana, Environment Statistics: Wildlife Digest 2014 — which reports populations and poaching, not disease, and is used as context only. Clinical summaries: WOAH and the MSD Veterinary Manual.

SmartHerd.ai flags behaviour. It does not diagnose, and it does not replace a veterinary officer or a herder.
